{\LARGE\bfseries\color{azulcmb} National Logistics Control\par}

{\large the Brazilian Mint as the public physical-digital trust anchor for the circulation of goods\par}

{\bfseries Miguel do Rosário}\\

{\small journalist}

Abstract. This paper introduces and substantiates the concept of National Logistics Control: the State's capacity to identify, authenticate and continuously track everything that enters, circulates within and leaves the national territory. The proposal is anchored in the Brazilian Mint (Casa da Moeda do Brasil), a state-owned company with more than three centuries of experience producing the Brazilian State's instruments of authenticity, acting as the issuer of physical-digital identities for products, cargoes and electronic seals, within an open, multi-vendor scheme. The methodology consists of comparative documentary research covering 23 countries and 40 certification operators, complemented by an analysis of Brazilian institutional assets. Results show that (i) most countries that deployed logistics control did so through state-owned or hybrid operators; (ii) operating systems produced measurable gains in revenue collection, transit time and diversion reduction; and (iii) Brazil currently holds all the necessary links — industrial, legal, logistical and fiscal — to lead Latin America in this field. The paper quantifies the scale of the opportunity, estimated at R\$ 500 billion per year of market exposed to informality, and presents a five-phase implementation roadmap derived from documented international experiences.

Keywords: national logistics control; traceability; electronic seals; chain of custody; Brazilian Mint; reindustrialization.

Introduction

World trade is undergoing, in this decade, a silent and profound transformation: that of verifiability. Products, cargoes and documents cease to be anonymous objects in transit and begin to carry verifiable digital identities, recorded events and cryptographic proofs of integrity. The European Union has made the Digital Product Passport mandatory, starting with batteries and reaching textiles, metals and electronics by the end of the decade. China and Hong Kong integrate their customs through mutually recognized electronic seals. The Eurasian Economic Union has turned the electronic seal into the object of an international treaty among five countries. In East Africa, four revenue authorities jointly operate a single satellite cargo-tracking platform.

There is a pattern in these movements: the control of the circulation of goods has ceased to be a mere fiscal technique and has become a strategic State function — trust infrastructure, as essential to the economy as currency, payment systems or property registries. And there is a second, less observed pattern: in most countries, the operator of this trust layer is a public company — frequently the national mint or security printer itself, as in Germany, South Korea, France and the Philippines.

This paper proposes that Brazil build its National Logistics Control: a public, open and multi-vendor system capable of knowing what enters, circulates within and leaves the country, in real time, and of conferring verifiable identity on every product and every cargo. It further demonstrates that the Brazilian institution fit to anchor this system already exists: the Casa da Moeda do Brasil (the Brazilian Mint), the state-owned company that produces the country's banknotes and coins, the Brazilian passport, the security seals of the electronic voting machines and the certification stamps for cigarettes and beverages — and which, in the latest fiscal year alone, produced 19 million conformity stamps verifiable by mobile phone.

The relevance of the topic is not merely technical. The Brazilian market exposed to illegal trade — smuggling, counterfeiting, piracy and cargo diversion — is estimated at R\$ 500 billion per year. This figure is not treated here as a lament: it is the measure of the market that can be formalized, of the revenue that can be recovered without creating new taxes, and of the national industry that can be protected and valorized. It is, therefore, the largest idle asset of the Brazilian economy — and the exact object of National Logistics Control.

Section 4 examines the international panorama, with complete comparative tables and an analysis of how other countries implemented their systems. Conceptual framework: traceability, chain of custody and trust infrastructure

The supply chain management literature has consolidated, over the past two decades, the distinction between logistics as physical movement and logistics as the integrated management of material, information and value flows. In this evolution, three concepts have become central to the engineering of national logistics systems.

The first is traceability: the capacity to reconstruct the history, application or location of an object by means of recorded identifications. The international reference standard for recording traceability events is the Electronic Product Code Information Services (EPCIS), maintained by the global standards organization GS1, which defines how to capture and share ``what, when, where and why'' events along the chain.

The second is the chain of custody: the chronological, documented and auditable sequence of possession, control, transfer and safekeeping of a physical asset. While traceability answers ``where has it been'', the chain of custody answers ``who was responsible for it, at each moment, and under which authorization''. It is the concept that turns logistics data into legal evidence.

The third — and the one this paper proposes as the original framing of the Brazilian problem — is trust infrastructure: the set of institutions, standards and public records that allows unrelated third parties to transact without the need for prior interpersonal trust. Currency is the canonical example: no one needs to know the payer to accept their money, because the State guarantees the banknote's authenticity. The thesis of this paper is that the circulation of goods can — and, in advanced countries, is already being — endowed with an analogous layer: a public system that guarantees the authenticity of products, seals and logistics events. In this design, the institution that certifies currency is the natural candidate to certify merchandise, because it already holds the industrial competence, the reputation and the mandate of authenticity.

From the normative standpoint, the technical literature records a relevant gap: existing standards cover mechanical seals (ISO 17712), active electronic seals (ISO 18185 series), the radio-frequency interface (ISO/IEC 18000-63) and RFID cryptography (ISO/IEC 29167), but no single standard fully covers a passive, single-use electronic seal with dual identity and custody registration. It was precisely for this reason that the Eurasian Economic Union published its own uniform requirements instead of waiting for an international standard — a precedent that grounds the proposition of a national certification scheme for Brazil.

Methodology

The research adopts the method of comparative documentary analysis, in three stages: (i) a survey of institutional certification models in 23 countries, classified by the nature of the main operator (state-owned, hybrid or private under contract); (ii) a survey of 40 companies and state-owned enterprises in the global security printing, identity and traceability sector, with their financial results, ownership structures and diversification strategies; and (iii) a synthesis of market, regulatory trends and implementation case studies. The sources are primary documents: management reports of state-owned companies, texts of laws and treaties, official communications from governments and revenue authorities, international technical standards and sector studies.

A three-level evidence discipline is adopted throughout the paper: verified data (from a cited primary source), estimate (explicitly marked as such) and proposition (an element of the proposed model, still to be validated in a pilot). No conclusion of this paper depends on unmarked extrapolation.

The choice of comparative study is justified by the object: National Logistics Control is a proposition of public policy and institutional engineering, whose appropriate scientific validation is the demonstration that (a) the model exists and operates in other jurisdictions, with measured results; (b) the necessary assets exist in Brazil; and (c) the proposed design is consistent with international technical standards. Nothing in the proposal is unprecedented in the world; the originality lies in the architecture of bringing together, under a single national standard anchored in the Brazilian Mint, elements that the reference countries have implemented separately.

The international panorama

State-owned trust operators: the rule, not the exception

Of the 23 countries mapped with certification and tracking systems, the main operator is state-owned in 61\% of cases and hybrid in 30\%; measured by covered population, state participation reaches 82\%. There is no national certification anywhere in the world outside the State context: the difference between countries is only who executes — and, in most cases, the executor is a public company, generally the national mint or security printer itself. Table~\ref{tab:estataisen} summarizes the most instructive cases.

Germany & Bundesdruckerei & 1.11 billion euros in revenue; profit multiplied sevenfold in four years & 92\% of revenue from digital solutions: identity, certificates and state cybersecurity; declared digital-sovereignty mission \\

South Korea & KOMSCO & record revenue, growing 26\% in the latest fiscal year & over 80\% of revenue outside currency: national mobile identity, the country's first public blockchain platform; exports security technology to more than 40 countries \\

France & IN Groupe & revenue almost tripled since its refounding; 24\% net margin & European digital-identity champion; acquired IDEMIA's identity division for about 1 billion euros; operates electronic freight transport information for the French government \\

Philippines & APO Production Unit & 31.2 billion tax stamps produced and tracked; about 1.3 billion dollars per year in associated revenue collection & the state printer operates the tax stamp system end-to-end, remunerated per stamp; self-sustaining, returns half its profit to the Treasury \\

The pattern is unequivocal: all these companies started from the same point — a documentary monopoly and a security-printing industrial plant, exactly the asset the Brazilian Mint possesses — and climbed the same value ladder: from the physical document to the digitalized stamp, from the stamp to the digital trust service, and from the service to technology exports. The German lesson is explicit: the same industrial base that prints money is the natural platform to certify the physical and digital worlds.

The second family of evidence consists of logistics control systems proper — platforms that track cargo through electronic seals, with results measured and published by the authorities themselves. Table~\ref{tab:sistemasen} consolidates the cases.

Three observations emerge from the comparison. First, logistics control is not expensive: the African regional system cost 4.4 million dollars for three countries — it was the previous fragmentation, with nine private operators charging up to 1,200 dollars per trip, that was expensive. Second, the winning design is the hybrid one with a public anchor: the public power defines the standard and accredits the device; the market competes in supply — as China–Hong Kong and Eurasia do. Third, Latin America is the only continent without any such system: what exists in the region is fiscal product traceability, not control of cargo in transit with certified seals and recognition between jurisdictions.

How countries implemented: lessons in deployment engineering

None of the reference systems was born from a single, nationwide deployment. All followed phased trajectories, which constitute the main practical lesson for Brazil. Table~\ref{tab:fasesen} summarizes the documented trajectories.

System & Trajectory & Lesson \\

Single E-lock (China–Hong Kong) & pilot in a single corridor; successive expansion to dozens of provinces, 93 clearance points and over a thousand routes; adherence always voluntary & start with one corridor and grow through demonstrated results, not obligation \\

RECTS (East Africa) & direct regional deployment, but on a single platform, satellite architecture and no fixed gates; replacement of fragmented private operators & a single public platform is cheaper and more effective than the sum of private solutions \\

Eurasia & treaty (in force for three years) $\rightarrow$ uniform technical requirements drafted with the private sector $\rightarrow$ national operator per country $\rightarrow$ mandatory phasing in three stages by product category & anchor the system in a high-level legal instrument and phase it by sector \\

Russia & mandatory labeling and traceability phased by category: tobacco, footwear, textiles, medicines, dairy, water & sector-by-sector phasing is the consolidated method of national deployment \\

Philippines (APO) & tax stamp system started just over a decade ago with one category and continuously expanded to new ones, always by the same state-owned company & start with a high-taxation sector and expand on the same platform \\

The convergence is remarkable: public standard first, pilot in a real corridor next, sector-by-sector scale afterwards, regional integration last. It is exactly this sequence that the roadmap in Section 8 transposes to Brazil.

The concept of National Logistics Control

Definition and scope

Definition. National Logistics Control is the State's capacity to know, with verifiable identity and continuous registration, everything that enters, circulates within and leaves the national territory — where certification (stamps, seals and digital identities) is the instrument that makes this knowledge possible.

The concept is organized in three moments, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fluxoen}: entry (ports, airports and borders, where cargo is identified and sealed upon arrival), circulation (highways, railways and warehouses, where integrity is verified at each passage) and exit (exports endowed with digital identity accepted in buying markets). Beneath the three moments, a single trust layer, operated by the Brazilian Mint: the secure identity of each product and cargo, the registration of each event and the national conformity mark.

Two technical clarifications preserve the realism of the proposal. First, the passive electronic seal does not transmit continuously: it provides visibility at each reading, and geolocation is supplied by readers installed along the corridors — exactly as the reference systems operate. Second, the seal does not physically prevent violation: it detects it, evidences it, hinders cargo substitution and assigns responsibility — and that is what reduces diversions by 76\% in the African corridor.

Institutional architecture: four state-owned companies, one system

National Logistics Control creates no new agency and removes no competence from any existing one. It brings together, under a single standard, capabilities that the Brazilian State already possesses, distributed across four links (Figure~\ref{fig:institucionalen}): the Brazilian Mint produces the stamps and seals and operates the identity and registration system; the Correios (the Brazilian postal service) distribute the certified seals in every municipality in the country and act as the registration authority at the front line — reproducing the model already tested with the electronic voting machine seals, which the Mint produces and Correios deliver nationwide, and the role that the Swiss and Italian postal services play as registration authorities for national digital identity; Infra S.A. integrates the control into corridors, ports and federal logistics planning systems; and the Receita Federal (the federal revenue service) regulates, inspects and collects, with real-time visibility of what circulates.

The market participates in open competition: any company, national or foreign, manufactures seals, smart labels and readers within the single standard, with no exclusivity and no hardware reservation — the same design as the China–Hong Kong customs administrations, which accredit the device and let the market supply it. Ceitec, the Brazilian state-owned information and communication technology company, participates as a technical consultancy for the selection of state-of-the-art chips and components among leading global suppliers.

Technical architecture: three layers, four certification objects

Technically, the system is organized in three layers that connect but do not overlap: (i) the certified electronic seal — the physical device, with tamper evidence, radio frequency and sensors, supplied by the market under a multi-vendor regime; (ii) the digital custody identity — the record associating seal, cargo, electronic tax documents, operators and events, issued and managed by the Brazilian Mint; and (iii) the electronic seal of the Brazilian Public Key Infrastructure — the digital stamp of legal entities on documents and event sets, under the responsibility of the National Institute of Information Technology, an instrument already present in Brazilian law.

Certification applies to four objects, not just the label: (1) the physical device — mechanical resistance, tamper evidence and impossibility of reconstitution; (2) the electronic identity and cybersecurity — unique cryptographic identifier, individualized keys, anti-cloning protection and immutable registration; (3) the platform and events — under the international EPCIS standard; and (4) the processes and operators — who may seal, open and inspect, and under which authorization.

Legal foundation and the gold mandate

The proposal requires, to begin with, no new legislation: the Brazilian Mint's bylaws authorize activities compatible with its industrial vocation, and authenticity certification is the company's historical core activity. But the National Congress is already conferring an even stronger foundation on the proposal: Bill 3.025/2023, approved by the Chamber of Deputies and under examination in the Senate, establishes the national gold traceability system and assigns to the Brazilian Mint, exclusively, the physical and digital marking of the metal and the management of its traceability, with fees provided by law to fund the operation. The Brazilian Legislature has therefore already recognized, in the case of gold, exactly the principle this paper proposes to generalize with method: the national mint as the physical-digital certifier of the country's value chains. The gold system is, by legislative design, the first real — and already funded — module of National Logistics Control.

Contributions to national development

Reindustrialization

A national standard of seals, smart labels, readers and platforms creates a stable, long-term anchor demand for the national industry of hardware, software and integration services — the same mechanism by which Bundesdruckerei pulled a German security-technology chain that today employs 1,300 people solely on the digitalization of the federal government. Since the scheme is open and multi-vendor, public demand converts into a competitive private market: national and foreign manufacturers compete for supply within the standard, with technology transfer. Certified traceability additionally protects formal industry against the predatory competition of illegal products — which today account for a market of R\$ 500 billion per year in the country — returning to Brazilian factories market shares now captured by smuggling and counterfeiting.

Reducing tax evasion and expanding revenue collection

The international evidence is direct: California's digital cigarette stamp added 133 million dollars per year in revenue; electronic cargo tracking increased tax revenue by 18\% in Kenya; the Philippine stamp system secures about 1.3 billion dollars annually; and fuel marking in the Philippines secured the equivalent of 1.028 trillion pesos over five years. The economic logic is that of data-driven enforcement: when every product and every cargo has a verifiable identity and recorded events, evasion ceases to be invisible. This is revenue recovered without creating taxes or raising rates — and, precisely for that reason, one of the highest public-return instruments available to the Brazilian State. The estimated fiscal multiplier for the Brazilian case — each real applied to the certification layer associated with dozens of reais in recovered revenue — is presented, duly qualified as an estimate, in Section 7.

Reducing waste and logistics losses

Circulation control attacks three measurable wastes. The first is time: border clearance fell from hours to minutes on the China–Hong Kong border, and transit in the Mombasa–Kampala corridor fell from six days to one and a half; transportation engineering studies indicate reductions of up to 50\% in border inspection time with electronic seals. The second is diversion and loss: the 76\% reduction in cargo diversion in East Africa stems from the immediate detection of violation and the assignment of responsibility at each link. The third is redundant bureaucracy: with a single seal recognized by all authorities, duplicated inspections and parallel operators are eliminated — the fragmentation that, in the African case, cost up to 1,200 dollars per trip.

Controlling the entry and exit of products

At entry, foreign cargo is identified and sealed at the port or border, and from then on circulates under continuous surveillance: what entered without identity is, by construction, illegal — and visible. At exit, each export shipment carries a digital identity verifiable by the buyer and the destination customs. The result is the closing of the complete cycle: the State gains real-time visibility of what enters, what circulates and what leaves — the very definition of National Logistics Control. It is worth noting that demand for this instrument already manifests itself in the administration: the federal revenue service itself has employed hundreds of thousands of electronic seals in recent operations, on a sectoral basis. What the proposal adds is the single national standard, interoperability and the public anchor.

Safety of products offered to Brazilians

Counterfeit or smuggled medicines, food, beverages, auto parts, toys and fuels are not only a fiscal problem: they are a health and public-safety problem. Identity verifiable by the consumer — as already occurs with the 19 million conformity stamps produced by the Brazilian Mint, verifiable by mobile phone — transfers to citizens the power to check the authenticity of what they buy, and to the State the power to withdraw irregular batches from circulation with surgical precision. The recorded chain of custody turns recalls, sanitary investigations and police actions into data-driven operations.

Inserting Brazil into new markets

The European Union will progressively require digital product passports for batteries, textiles, metals, electronics and other sectors. Every Brazilian exporter will need a digital product identity — and someone will issue and anchor it. If this anchor is Brazilian, the country converts an external regulatory requirement into competitive advantage: the national product leaves the port already carrying the identity accepted at destination, without foreign intermediaries charging for conformity certification. There is, additionally, an opportunity for continental leadership: Latin America is the only continent without a regional cargo-control system, and Brazil is building the physical corridors — the South American Integration Routes, with about two hundred works, and the bioceanic connections to the Pacific — which lack exactly the digital trust layer proposed here. The Eurasian treaty model shows the way: uniform requirements and one national operator per country, with mutual recognition across borders. Brazil can be the continent's trust anchor, through Mercosur.

Valorizing Brazilian products

Certification of origin, authenticity and custody is a commercial asset: it differentiates Brazilian products in demanding markets, sustains premium prices and protects geographical indications and national brands. South Korea turned its mint into an exporter of trust technology to more than 40 countries; France turned its own into a European identity champion. The same movement is within Brazil's reach: coffee, meats, minerals, gold and manufactures with a chain of custody certified by a state-owned company with more than three centuries of credibility are not merely tracked products — they are valorized products, with origin turned into a selling argument.

Market dimension and economic model

The global market for tax stamps and traceability is estimated at 2.5 billion dollars per year, projected to reach 4.16 billion by the end of the decade, growing 8.7\% per year. Upon this market, an accelerated consolidation by international funds is taking place: the authentication portfolio of one of the world's oldest banknote printers was absorbed by an American company with a declared goal of tripling revenue; the European identity leader was sliced by funds and resold to national buyers — including the French state-owned company itself. The trust layer of economies is being priced as a strategic asset — and countries that outsourced it are now discussing bringing it back for national security reasons. Brazil has the opportunity to build its own with a public anchor from the initial design, without closing the market: open standard, multiple suppliers, and keys and data under Brazilian governance.

The domestic dimension of the opportunity needs no optimistic projections: the Brazilian market exposed to illegal trade is estimated at R\$ 500 billion per year. This amount is not revenue capturable by any company — it is the universe of harm that National Logistics Control addresses. The conversion chain is conservative: of the total harm, a fraction is addressable by traceability; of that, a fraction converts into recovered revenue; and of the latter, a share remunerates the scheme. Sectoral estimates point to potential fiscal recovery in the range of tens of billions of reais per year and recurring revenue for the scheme operator on the order of R\$ 100 to 300 million per year [ESTIMATE] — sufficient, by itself, to fund the entire operation under the Philippine model, in which the state-owned company sustains itself with a per-stamp fee and still returns half its profit to the Treasury. The scheme's revenue sources are multiple and all have international precedent: licensing of the conformity mark, cryptographic personalization fees, type certification, audits and micro-fees per query and validation — to which is added, as the first concrete revenue, the gold system fee provided for in Bill 3.025/2023.

Discussion: risks and safeguards

Four risks deserve explicit treatment, because they determine the correct design of the system.

Sovereignty of data and keys. The integral outsourcing of the trust layer has already produced, worldwide, a paradigmatic case: one of the largest global suppliers of fiscal technology was convicted by the Swiss justice to pay 81 million francs for organizational failures that allowed the bribery of public agents in operations in South America. The conclusion drawn by the reference countries was not to close the market — it was to keep keys, records and audit under a public anchor, with the market competing in device supply. That is the design proposed here.

Conflict between manufacturing and certifying. The separation between scheme management and device manufacturing eliminates the conflict: the Brazilian Mint administers identities, registration and the conformity mark; accredited independent laboratories perform the tests; the market manufactures. This is the China–Hong Kong customs model.

Technological realism. The system must not promise what physics does not deliver: a passive seal does not transmit continuously, and no device is ``tamper-proof'' — it is tamper-evident. Technical honesty is, in fact, a competitive advantage: the reference systems operate exactly this way, with documented results.

Institutional reversibility. Trust systems require stability; that is why countries anchor them in law or treaty, not in administrative acts — as Eurasia did with its treaty and as Bill 3.025/2023 does in Brazil. The proposed deployment trajectory respects this lesson: each phase is consolidated in its own legal instrument before advancing.

Concluding remarks

The world is building, country by country and bloc by bloc, the trust layer of the circulation of goods. The comparative evidence gathered in this paper shows that the model is known, tested and measured; that the best operators are state-owned security printers that converted the authenticity monopoly into national digital platforms; and that Latin America is the only continental space still empty. Brazil holds all the links of the system — the public certifier with more than three centuries of credibility and industrial-scale production, the distribution network present in every municipality, the most advanced electronic tax documents in the world, the logistics corridors under construction and the first legislative traceability mandate already designed in the gold system. National Logistics Control is, therefore, less an invention than a bringing together — of existing capabilities, under a public and open standard, anchored in the Brazilian Mint — with direct contributions to reindustrialization, revenue collection, logistics efficiency, consumer safety, external insertion and the valorization of national production. Future research should concentrate on the design of the real-corridor pilot and on the independent measurement of its effects, in the form of the roadmap proposed here.
